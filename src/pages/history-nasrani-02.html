The Portuguese Catholics and Syrian Orthodox

This flourishing of the Indian Christian community continued until the sixteenth century, when Portuguese Catholic presence increased in the region coupled with diminishing influence of the Church of the East. The Portuguese Catholics were fiercely opposed to the practices descended from the Church of the East, partly due to the Nestorian nature, and also to the lack of allegiance to the Roman Pope. These tensions culminated in the Synod of Diamper, which forcibly brought the Malayalee Christians of the Archdiocese of Angamaly under the Latin Archdiocese of Goa. This led to the latinization of Malayalee Christian practices, which had been preserved for multiple centuries. Additionally, the autonomy the community had enjoyed for over a millennium was being infringed upon, as matters of governance were required to be submitted to foreign authorities. In opposition to this, many clergy and laity took part in the Coonan Cross Oath, by which they pledged on a cross that they would resist latinization and return to ancient practices. In doing so, they were assisted by Syriac Orthodox Church, who sent Bishops to strengthen the community, leading to the start of Orthodox Christianity in Kerala.

Present Day

Despite the tumultuous struggle Christians have faced in India, it is by the Holy Spirit that the community was able to continue, eventually culminating in the adoption of the Orthodox faith. This adoption brought the Church in line with other Ancient Christian faiths located around the world, such as the Coptic and Armenian Orthodox Churches. However, despite this adoption of faith and theology, the traditions of the Church have remained steadfast over time, preserving identity and heritage, thus making Christianity inherently native to India.

— Neil Mathew
