The Promises No One Speaks

What the Faithful Quietly Expect of Their Shepherds

Rev. Fr. Alexander James Kurien

Senior Orthodox Priest
Senior Executive, United States Government, Washington, DC

In thirty-nine years of priesthood, no parishioner has ever sat across from me and listed what they expect of me. No one hands a new priest a contract. No one reads the terms aloud at his ordination. Yet the terms are there. They are written in the eyes of the widow who waits by the door after the Holy Qurbana, in the silence of the young man who lingers in the last pew, in the voice of the mother who calls late at night and apologizes for calling at all. The faithful carry expectations of their spiritual leaders that they rarely voice but always feel, and by those expectations they quietly and honestly measure whether the shepherd can be trusted.

I have come to believe that most departures from a church, most cooling of faith, and most slow drifting away begin not with a quarrel over doctrine but with one of these unspoken promises being broken. Not shouted about. Noted and remembered. So let me try to say aloud what the faithful so seldom say.

The First Expectation: That You Will Be There

Our Lord called Himself the Good Shepherd who knows His sheep by name and whose sheep know His voice (John 10:14). Notice the order. Knowing comes before leading. The faithful do not first ask whether their priest is eloquent, learned, or gifted in administration. They ask, without words, “Will he come when I need him?”

Every priest knows the call that comes from a hospital corridor. Every priest knows the funeral that disrupts a carefully planned week. In those hours, a sermon is not required. Presence is. I have learned that people forget most of what I preach, but they never forget who stood beside the bed when their father drew his last breath. The ministry of presence is not a small part of the priesthood. It may well be the largest. When a shepherd is too busy to come, the flock rarely complains. They conclude they were never truly known, and people do not stay long where they are not known.

The Second Expectation: That You Will Live What You Preach

St. Peter, writing to the elders of the early Church, urged them to shepherd the flock not as lords over those in their charge but as examples to the flock (1 Peter 5:3). St. Paul told young Timothy to be an example to the believers in word, conduct, love, spirit, faith, and purity (1 Timothy 4:12).

The faithful listen to our homilies, but they watch our lives. They notice how we speak to the altar server who made a mistake. They notice whether we are generous or grasping, whether we hold grudges or forgive, and whether we kneel in prayer when no one is watching. A child in the third row may not remember the theology of the Incarnation, but he will remember whether his priest was kind.

St. John Chrysostom, who wrote so beautifully about the dignity of the priesthood, warned that a priest’s faults cannot remain hidden because he stands on a height where every soul can see him. It is a frightening and holy truth. Our integrity is never a private matter. It is the soil in which the faith of others either grows or withers.
